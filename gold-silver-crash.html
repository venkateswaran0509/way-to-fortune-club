Gold and Silver Crash: Why Are Precious Metal Prices Falling So Sharply?

Gold and silver have witnessed a sharp correction after reaching record highs in January 2026. But the fall in silver has been significantly steeper.

Gold declined from its January COMEX peak of approximately $5,625 to an intraday low near $3,783 in November. Silver fell nearly 61% from its January record of $121.65, trading around $46 per ounce on November 18.

So, what is driving this correction?

1. The Federal Reserve and the US Dollar

Expectations of higher US interest rates have put pressure on precious metals. When interest rates and bond yields rise, non-interest-bearing assets such as gold and silver become relatively less attractive.

A stronger US dollar also makes precious metals more expensive for buyers using other currencies.

However, interest-rate expectations alone do not explain the magnitude of this correction.

2. Leverage and Forced Selling

Silver's sharp fall was amplified by leveraged positions in the futures market.

When prices started falling, traders facing margin calls were forced to liquidate their positions. Exchange margin increases added further pressure.

This created a chain reaction: falling prices triggered forced selling, which pushed prices lower and triggered more selling.

This was particularly severe in silver, which is generally more volatile than gold.

3. High Prices Started Hurting Physical Demand

Another important factor is demand destruction.

According to the research figures, global gold jewellery demand fell 17% to 313 tonnes in Q3 2026. India's gold demand also declined 29% to 162.5 tonnes during the same quarter.

Silver faced pressure from the industrial side as well. Solar-related silver demand is estimated to have declined approximately 19%, partly because manufacturers are reducing silver usage and adopting alternatives.

Interestingly, the silver market still recorded an estimated 35-million-ounce supply deficit. But a supply deficit does not automatically guarantee rising prices, especially when investment demand and physical consumption weaken.

4. What About Central Banks and Industrial Demand?

Central-bank gold purchases remain an important long-term factor, but buying has not been uniform. Some central banks have also sold gold or used it in foreign-exchange transactions.

Similarly, silver's industrial demand story is real, but it is not strong enough to ignore the impact of high prices, substitution and leveraged selling.

The important distinction is that long-term demand drivers do not prevent short-term market corrections.

6. What Happens Next?

The next major event to watch is the US Federal Reserve's December 9 meeting.

If the Fed signals further rate increases, precious metals could face additional pressure. If rate expectations stabilise or shift towards a pause, gold and silver could see a recovery.

Historical corrections also show that recovery timelines can vary dramatically. Silver took decades to regain its 1980 peak, while the 2020 COVID-related crash recovered much faster.

The takeaway: Gold and silver are not falling because their long-term stories have disappeared. They are facing a combination of higher-rate expectations, leveraged liquidation and weaker physical demand. Whether this correction becomes an opportunity or continues into a deeper decline will depend on how these factors evolve.
